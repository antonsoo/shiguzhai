# Ten bells, and a choice about what counts

This is a source-based listening exhibit for English-speaking readers. It uses
the ten bells found together at Shiguzhai in June 1993 and measured in 2022,
as published by Li et al. in 2024. The reader should leave understanding why
one bell can contribute different pitches, why a reported B-tone is sometimes
a collection of observations, and why assigning those observations to modern
piano keys does not establish an ancient equal-tempered scale.

## Visual plan

Use the actual photographed bronze as the opening object. A close photograph
and its measured pitch pair share the opening spread; the ten-row frequency
plot becomes the main working surface below it. A cool mineral palette makes
the blue-grey photographs feel at home without imitating parchment or bronze
texture. White gives the measurement surface clarity.

Review against generic alternatives: the opening photograph is evidence, not
a decorative hero; the chart's points are actual reported frequencies, not
an invented ancient scale; the two colors encode strike positions everywhere.
There are no card grids, faux bronze textures, ornamental motion, or anonymous
temple-like icons. The shape of this page follows its specific question.

## Boundaries

All sounds are short modern sine tones at the selected published frequency.
They are neither museum recordings nor a physical simulation of bell timbre.
The strike diagram indicates locations schematically; it is not a vibration
simulation. The source's distinct-frequency lists contain neither frequency occurrence
counts nor strike order, and must never become histograms or probabilities.

The two comparison switches make explicit analytical choices: include/exclude
the differently decorated bell III, and retain one/all reported frequencies.
The first A entry is just the first listed. The first B entry is described by
the authors as the clearest or most often occurring. These are not synonyms
for an intended ancient tuning. The app uses the original integer hertz values
and computes modern note labels independently; it does not replace measurements
with the source's rounded note labels.

## Reference-grid refinement

Source review exposed an important boundary: the 269 Hz observation lies close
to the nearest-key threshold at the study's 440 Hz reference. Add a native
430–450 Hz slider for the modern grid. Keep the marks on a fixed logarithmic
frequency axis while the grid moves. This should make the choice visible without
silently changing the evidence. The app opens at 440 Hz and offers an explicit
reset to the study's reference. The range is an analytical demonstration, never
a proposed ancient tuning range. Shared links, exports, comparison tones and
coverage counts retain the chosen reference.
