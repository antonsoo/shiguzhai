# Evidence and interpretation

The exhibit asks a narrow question: what can a reader hear and compare from the
reported frequencies of ten surviving bells? It does not generate an imagined
ancient song or assign modern piano tuning to ancient makers.

## Measured objects

The complete evidence chain is Li et al. (2024),
[DOI 10.1186/s40494-024-01377-0](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40494-024-01377-0), Table 1
on page 6, the discussion on pages 7–12, and figures 1, 2, 3 and 5. The full paper
and table are bundled, with their licenses described in [THIRD_PARTY.md](../THIRD_PARTY.md).

The app uses columns CsM I through X, the ten bells found together in June 1993.
The source assigns their manufacture to the twelfth century BCE. I–X are the
corresponding author's new numbers, not inscriptions on the bells. Their museum
accessions provide a second identity. The table's two preceding objects are
outside this comparison. The 1959 and August 1993 finds remain separate.

For every bell, both frequency lists preserve the source order and integer hertz
values. The first B entry is explicitly the clearest or most often occurring;
the first A entry has no equivalent explicit priority statement. No missing
measurement is interpolated. Dimensions are centimeters, as the table states.

The table notes give August 2022, Changsha Museum, 22 degrees Celsius, with the
bells standing on their shanks. The method describes three strikes at each of two
A positions and four B positions; the discussion allows additional B strikes.
Reported lists omit repeated frequencies. They therefore supply neither counts
of each frequency's occurrence nor the chronology of the strikes. A histogram,
probability or confidence interval cannot be recovered from these lists.

## What the controls do

| Control                         | Calculation                                     | Historical boundary                                                       |
| ------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Select bell / A / B             | Choose an actual source integer                 | No continuous retuning of a reported measurement                          |
| Same find / similar decoration  | Keep ten bells or exclude CsM III               | An explicit grouping choice, not a verdict on ancient ensemble membership |
| Leading entries / all values    | Plot 20 or 87 entries for all ten bells         | Leading is not a claim about intended tuning or probability               |
| Modern reference, A4 430–450 Hz | Move a twelve-tone equal-tempered grid          | An analytical choice, not a historical pitch range                        |
| Hear every B value              | Play each distinct B entry once, in table order | A listening comparison, not a melody or a strike sequence                 |

The plot's frequency coordinates stay fixed when the reference slider moves.
Its grid and nearest-key labels change. At 440 Hz, retaining all observations
adds a nearest-C4 entry: 269 Hz. This value lies 1.8768 cents below the boundary
with C-sharp4, at approximately 269.292 Hz. At a 439 Hz reference the same value
is nearest C-sharp4. The published integer is used as given; its original
rounding and measurement uncertainty are not known. The display must not turn
that classification threshold into a claim about a newly established ancient note.

All listening uses bounded sine tones, a short amplitude envelope and explicit
user initiation. No museum recording, bell timbre, ancient temperament or
reconstructed performance is claimed. The source's interpretation of a musical
set is attributed to the authors; the controls expose assumptions that a reader
can change independently.
